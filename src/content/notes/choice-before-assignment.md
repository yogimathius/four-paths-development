title: Choice before assignment

Chore Credits started with a household problem that software often treats as an assignment problem: a parent distributes work, a child completes it, and the app records the transaction. The harder part is usually the agreement underneath. Who gets to choose? What counts as done? What happens when a day is unusually difficult? Is the rule fair to children of different ages?

An early answer was physical: a board visible on the wall or fridge. It made the agreement part of the room rather than a notification on a phone. The app then had a clearer job. It could keep the private record of available work, claims, exceptions, approvals and weekly settlement, while the board kept the shared arrangement visible.

Children choose from useful work they are eligible to do. The credit levels are bounded, and a chore has a concrete finish line. Submitted work is not quietly treated as approved work; the distinction remains visible. A parent can review exceptions without needing to supervise every routine action.

Fairness here is not a single leaderboard or identical chores for everyone. Age, supervision, excused days and exclusive claims matter. The design has to make those rules comprehensible without turning the family into an administrative office. The current closed test is less about whether the software can track a chore than whether it gives children more genuine agency and parents less daily management.
